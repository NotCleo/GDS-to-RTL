EASTER EGG 08
THE ARCHITECTURE IS DRAWN ON THE DIE: 11 + 11 + 1
======================================================================

WHAT
  The blog post says "the circuit is physically arranged to hint at its
  functionality, so look closely at the layout". The arrangement is clear
  once you know which cells to look at.

  Every one of the 23 two-bit counters in this chip sits in a single narrow
  vertical column, and they are stacked in three groups with a visible gap
  between the two big ones:

      11 column counters      y = 185.0 .. 285.6
      a gap                   y = 146.9 .. 185.0
      11 region counters      y =  49.0 .. 146.9
       1 row counter          off to the side at x = 80.5, y = 103.4

  Eleven of one group, eleven of another, one of a third: eleven column
  counters, eleven region counters and one shared row counter. That is the
  architecture of the checker, readable off the floorplan alone.

WHERE
  puzzle/puzzle.gds, in the placement coordinates of the flip-flops.

  The counter column occupies x = 114.8 .. 126.3 um of a 200 um wide die, so
  the whole checker is 11.5 um across on a die 17 times wider than that.
  Everything else on the die is the input handling, the scan position
  counter and the output generator.

  Full table, with each counter's die coordinate and how many grid cells it
  watches, in puzzle-solution/06_region_map.txt.

HOW
  Not by looking at the GDS. I tried that first and it gives nothing: 9,875
  placements, none labelled, and no way to tell one flip-flop from another
  by eye.

  What made the hint readable was doing it in the other order. Recover the
  netlist first, find the counters, and only then go back to the geometry.

      1. Extract the netlist from the polygons.  738 nets, 728 cells.
      2. Build the register graph: one node per flip-flop, an edge a -> b
         when Q(a) is in the combinational fan-in of D(b). Run Tarjan.
         26 feedback groups come back, 23 of them pairs of flops in a tight
         loop, all the same shape. Two-bit counters.
      3. My extractor numbers instances uNNN by their position in the GDS
         reference list, so uNNN back to (x, y) is a lookup, not a search.
         Look up all 46 flops.

  With those 23 coordinates on screen the arrangement was clear. They are
  not scattered through the logic, which is where an unconstrained placer
  would put them; they form a vertical stack in two blocks of eleven, with
  one placed away from the rest.

  That is what indicated a third constraint family I had not accounted for.
  At that point my working hypothesis was two stars per row
  and per column with no two touching, which needs 22 counters at most and
  arguably only 12. Twenty-three counters in an 11 + 11 + 1 arrangement does
  not fit that story. Something was counting eleven of a thing that was
  neither a row nor a column.

  It also explains the asymmetry that had been bothering me. Eleven rows,
  eleven columns and eleven regions suggests 33 counters, and there are 23.
  The missing ten are the rows: the grid streams in row-major at one cell per
  clock, so only one row is ever in flight, and a single counter cleared at
  each row boundary serves all eleven. Columns and regions are interleaved
  across the whole frame, so each needs a counter that persists. So the
  floorplan gives the input format as well as the rule set.

  The probing that came next confirmed all of it electrically: 11 counters
  watch a column each, 11 watch an irregular blob each, and the 23rd
  watches every cell but is cleared eleven times a frame.

WHEN
  Day 3, after the register graph was working and before the region map was
  read out. This is the one easter egg that helped solve the puzzle: it
  stopped further work on a two-constraint hypothesis that had already been
  falsified.
