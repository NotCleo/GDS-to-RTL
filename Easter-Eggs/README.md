# Easter eggs

Jane Street said they hid "a few fun Easter eggs in the circuit and in the
repository". Here are the eight I found, what each one is, and when and how I
tripped over it. None of them are needed to solve the puzzle.

Everything below is regenerated by `bash run.sh eggs` into the three text files
in this folder. Nothing here is asserted by hand.

---

## 1. The Jane Street logo, etched in metal 2

| | |
|---|---|
| where | met2 (layer 69/20), a 17.1 um square at x 34.9 to 52.0, y 35.2 to 52.3 |
| what | 1,366 sub-micron polygons, electrically floating, connected to nothing |
| found | day 4, during the layer sweep |

I actually saw this on day one and did not register it. When I first opened the
GDS in a 3D viewer there was a pale ring low on the die, and I filed it away as
some via array I did not understand yet.

What made it findable properly was asking a duller question: at the top level of
a routed design, geometry is either long power straps or short routing jogs. So

## 2. Morse code under the die

| | |
|---|---|
| where | layer 200/0, 36 bars in a strip at y = -52.72 um, below the die outline |
| what | `PER ARENAM AD ASTRA` |
| found | day 4, by diffing the puzzle's layer set against the warm-up's |

This is the one I am happiest about, because I found it without knowing there
was anything to find.

Both designs went through the same flow, so any layer present in one and not the
other was put there deliberately. That diff gives three entries, and two of them
turned out to be boring: 66/15 and 81/23 appear only because the puzzle
instantiates `conb_1` and `diode_2` cells and the warm-up does not. They live
inside standard cells, so they are a library difference, not decoration.

The third, layer 200/0, is not a sky130 mask layer at all. It carries two cells,
`INTERNAL_3` and `INTERNAL_7`, placed 36 times in a horizontal strip below the
die where nothing is ever fabricated. The two cells are 1.38 um and 4.14 um wide,
exactly 1:3, and the gaps between placements come in three sizes with ratios
1:3:7.

Short mark, long mark, three gap lengths at 1/3/7. That is International Morse
timing, and it decodes cleanly:

```
widths : . - - . . . - . . - . - . . - . . - - - . - - . . . - . . . - . - . . -
gaps   : 1 1 1 3 3 1 1 7 1 3 1 1 3 3 1 3 1 3 1 7 1 3 1 1 7 1 3 1 1 3 3 1 1 3 1
MORSE  : PER ARENAM AD ASTRA
```

Latin, roughly "through the sand to the stars", playing on *per aspera ad astra*.
Sand is silicon and the puzzle is about stars, so it works twice.

## 3. "Leave no stone unturned"

| | |
|---|---|
| where | the `$version` field of `example_inputs.vcd` |
| what | `Leave no stone unturned! But for this file, consider looking at it in a waveform viewer instead.` |
| found | embarrassingly late, day 4 |

No tool writes a `$version` field like that. It is a note left for a human.

I found it late for a stupid reason: I had been feeding the VCD to a parser and
to Surfer since day two, and never once opened it in a text editor. The line is
sitting in the first ten lines of the file, telling you to go and look at the
waveform, which is where egg 5 is hiding.

## 4. A timestamp that never existed on most clocks

| | |
|---|---|
| where | the `$date` field of `example_inputs.vcd` |
| what | `Sat Dec 31 23:59:60 2016` |
| found | same moment as egg 3, two lines above it |

Second 60. That is a leap second, and it is a real one: 31 December 2016 is the
most recent leap second ever inserted into UTC. It is also exactly the kind of
timestamp that quietly breaks date parsers, which I assume is the joke.

## 5. A message hidden in the sample inputs

| | |
|---|---|
| where | the `I` input of `example_inputs.vcd`, both frames |
| what | `The night sky awaits` |
| found | day 4, after egg 3 told me to look |

The sample waveform is described as "some inputs being fed to the design
(unfortunately, not the correct inputs to make `success` go high!)". For three
days I read that as a failed solve attempt and ignored it.

It is not a failed attempt. Two things give it away before you decode anything.
Both frames contain exactly 38 stars, which is too identical to be random. And
in both frames, columns 7 through 10 are empty in every single row.

Eleven rows, seven usable columns. Seven bits is one ASCII character. Read each
row least-significant-bit first and both frames turn into text:

```
. . * . * . * . . . .   0010101 ->  84 -> 'T'
. . . * . * * . . . .   0001011 -> 104 -> 'h'
* . * . . * * . . . .   1010011 -> 101 -> 'e'
```

Frame 0 gives `The night s`, frame 1 gives `ky awaits  `.

Which means the puzzle told me it was about stars on the very first day, in the
one file I had decided was a dead end.

## 6. Three verdicts nobody was meant to see

| | |
|---|---|
| where | the output ROM, the block the hint image says to ignore |
| what | `EMPTY SKY`, `BIG BANG`, `TRY AGAIN` |
| found | day 4, after solving, by deliberately failing |

Once the chip was answering correctly I got curious about what else it could
say, so I swept the star count and read `O[7:0]` on each run:

| what I fed it | what it printed |
|---|---|
| no stars at all | `EMPTY SKY` |
| far too many stars | `BIG BANG` |
| a wrong but plausible grid | `TRY AGAIN` |
| the answer | `(* TWO STARS *)` |

Four messages sitting in silicon, three of which exist purely to be funny at
someone who got it wrong.

## 7. The answer is itself a joke

| | |
|---|---|
| where | `O[7:0]`, streamed one character per clock after `success` rises |
| what | `(* TWO STARS *)` |
| found | day 4, the moment it worked |

`(* ... *)` is Verilog attribute syntax. Inside it is the rule of the puzzle:
two stars. So the final answer is a piece of hardware-description-language
punctuation wrapped around the constraint the hardware spends 728 gates
checking. I laughed out loud at this one.

## 8. The architecture is drawn on the die

| | |
|---|---|
| where | the physical placement of the flip-flops, x 114.8 to 126.3 |
| what | 11 + 11 + 1 |
| found | day 3, and this one actually helped me solve it |

The blog post says "the circuit is physically arranged to hint at its
functionality, so look closely at the layout". It is not kidding, but the hint is
invisible until you have recovered enough structure to know which flops matter.

Once I had found the counter pairs, I looked up their die coordinates instead of
reading any more logic. They form one vertical stack of identical two-bit
slices, in three groups:

| group | count | y range |
|---|---|---|
| column counters | 11 | 185.0 to 285.6 |
| gap | | 147 to 185 |
| region counters | 11 | 49.0 to 146.9 |
| row counter, off to the side at x 80.5 | 1 | 103.4 |

Eleven of one thing, eleven of another, one of a third, with a visible gap
separating the two big groups. That is the whole architecture, readable off a
floorplan before understanding a single gate. It is what told me there was a
third constraint family I had not accounted for.
