EASTER EGG 09
THE FIVE MESSAGES IN THE OUTPUT ROM
======================================================================

WHAT
  The block the hint image labels "output generator, safe to ignore at
  first" is a ROM of English sentences. There are exactly five, and the
  chip picks one based on how you got it wrong:

      EMPTY SKY          you sent 121 zeros
      BIG BANG           you sent 121 ones
      TRY AGAIN          any ordinary wrong grid
      TWO NOT TOUCH      every count correct, and two stars touching
      (* TWO STARS *)    the one grid that satisfies every rule

  The fourth is the least reachable.

  TWO NOT TOUCH is the other name of Star Battle. To reach it you have to
  send a grid that gets every count right, two stars in every row, two in
  every column, two in every one of the eleven irregular regions, 22 stars
  in total, and then break exactly one rule: two stars are adjacent. The
  chip names the rule that was broken, and that rule is the name of the
  puzzle.

  The fifth is (* ... *), which is Verilog attribute syntax and also an
  OCaml comment. Inside it is the rule the 728 gates check over the frame:
  TWO STARS.

WHERE
  O[7:0], streamed one ASCII character per clock starting on the 122nd
  enabled rising edge, the same edge on which success resolves.

  Everything is in puzzle-solution/10_message_catalogue.txt, including a
  grid that triggers each message.

HOW
  This came out of the testbench work, and I nearly stopped one message
  short.

  The first version was guesswork. Once the chip was answering correctly I
  drove it with the four cases I could think of, read O[7:0] on each, and
  found four messages: nothing, everything, something wrong, and the
  answer. I wrote that up as the catalogue.

  Four messages was not a measurement of the ROM, only a list of the grids
  I had tried. So I replaced it with an enumeration:

      1. Unroll the netlist from reset over 125 steps with all 121 input
         bits left free.
      2. Ask the SAT solver to enumerate every value the output bus can take
         on the first output edge. Four come back: '(', 'B', 'E', 'T'.
      3. For each of those, ask it to enumerate every value the bus can take
         on the second edge given the first. 'T' splits into 'R' and 'W'.
         The others do not split.
      4. Two characters separate every message, so when the enumeration
         returns UNSAT the catalogue is closed. Five two-character prefixes,
         fourteen SAT queries, the last one UNSAT.
      5. Each prefix hands back the grid that produced it. Simulate that
         grid through the gates to read the rest of the string.

  The 'T' split is where the fifth message came from. One branch is TRY
  AGAIN, which I already had. The other returned a grid that drives the
  gates to spell TWO NOT TOUCH.

  Then I worked out what class of grid that was. The grid the solver found
  has 22 stars, two in every row, two in every column, two in every region,
  and eleven adjacent pairs. To confirm the trigger rather than assume it, I
  asked z3 for 40 more grids in the same class, satisfy every count and
  touch somewhere, and drove all 40 through the netlist:

      40 of 40  ->  TWO NOT TOUCH,  success = 0

  and, as a control, 20 grids that are two per row, two per column and
  no-touch but wrong on regions:

      20 of 20  ->  TRY AGAIN,      success = 0

  So the condition is exact: every count right, adjacency wrong.

  A random sweep does not reach this verdict. Drop 22 stars at random on
  121 cells and the chance of hitting two per row and two per column and
  two per region is negligible: I ran 60 such grids and all 60 came back
  TRY AGAIN. I ran 60 more built to have two stars in every row and every
  column by construction, and all 60 still came back TRY AGAIN, because
  none of them also got the regions right. The class has to be solved for.

  Finding it also meant my recovered RTL was wrong. It had four verdicts,
  and the 564-grid equivalence run had passed only because none of its grids
  reached the fifth case. So the RTL got a fifth verdict, and 24 z3-built
  counts-right-and-touching grids were added to the vector set:

      EQUIVALENCE: 0 success mismatches, 0 O mismatches over 564 grids

  An equivalence run is only as good as its vectors, and these vectors were
  extended by a solver result rather than by guesswork.

WHEN
  Day 4, after the puzzle was solved, while writing the equivalence
  testbench. The four-message version was day 4 as well; the fifth message
  came out of replacing the guessing with enumeration, which is the last
  thing I did to this pipeline.
