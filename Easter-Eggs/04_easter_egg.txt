EASTER EGG 04
A TIMESTAMP THAT MOST CLOCKS NEVER SHOWED
======================================================================

WHAT
  The $date field of the sample waveform reads:

      Sat Dec 31 23:59:60 2016

  Second sixty. On the face of it that is a broken timestamp, and most date
  parsers will refuse it, because most date libraries believe a minute has
  sixty seconds numbered 0 to 59.

  It is not broken. It is a leap second, and it is a real one. 31 December
  2016 at 23:59:60 UTC is the most recent leap second ever inserted into
  UTC: the IERS added one that night, so that minute genuinely had 61
  seconds. It is also, as it happens, a Saturday, so even the weekday is
  right.

  So the joke has two layers. It is a timestamp that never existed on the
  computer you are reading it on, and it is a timestamp that did exist on
  the planet.

WHERE
  puzzle/example_inputs.vcd, lines 1 to 3, immediately above egg 03:

      $date
        Sat Dec 31 23:59:60 2016
      $end

HOW
  Same moment as egg 03, two lines above it. Once you have decided to open
  the VCD in a text editor instead of only in a waveform viewer, both are
  free.

  What made it register rather than slide past is that I had just read the
  $version line telling me to leave no stone unturned, so I was reading the
  header carefully instead of skimming to $enddefinitions. The :60 stands
  out if you are looking at the characters rather than at the shape of a
  date.

  Checking it was one search: leap seconds are a short list, 27 of them
  since 1972, and 2016-12-31 is the last entry on it.

WHEN
  Day 1, the same minute as egg 03. The pipeline prints it every run, in
  puzzle-solution/04_vcd_replay.txt, next to the replay result, because the
  two facts belong together: this is a recording of a real chip, and
  somebody dated it with a second that most software says cannot exist.
